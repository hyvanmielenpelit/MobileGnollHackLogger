How the graders work

Who scores, who checks, who writes the reports, which models to use, and how to choose the settings.

1. The grading roles

Role Scores? What it does
Assessor Yes Grades each answer against its rubric: four dimension levels (accuracy, completeness, conciseness, readability), a quality score and a critical-error flag. In a single-assessor run its verdict is the score
Co-Assessor Yes Makes the run a panel. Grades every answer exactly as the assessor does, blind to it; the published score is the mean of the two. Must come from a different provider than the assessor, and neither may be the model under test
Second Reader No Single-assessor runs. A different model grades a chosen set of answers again, independently, to check the assessor
Reference Reader No Panel runs. The same slot, reading every answer, as a third family's check on the panel
Claim Verifier No When a grader says a fact in an answer is wrong or unverifiable, looks it up in the game source and wiki and rules it true, false or undecided. Shows where a grader was wrong; changes no score
Report writer No Writes the Executive Summary, Technical Report and Internal Brief from computed figures; changes no score. Chosen in Model Comparison's Report Pack dialog, not on a run

The run's final summary (synthesis) is written by the assessor, and in a panel run by each member. It uses the same model and needs no choice of its own.

Before 2026-09-27 this role was called the "second opinion". Reports exported before then, and the API and database field names, still use that name.

2. The Second Reader (single-assessor runs)

What question it answers. "Would an independent grader reach the same verdict on this answer?" Over a run: "How reliable is our grading?"

When it runs. In the grading pipeline of each answer, after that answer's assessment and claim check. Which answers it reads is set by Coverage (section 3). Under Flagged answers and statistical outliers, a final sweep runs after every answer is scored.

What it sees. The question, the rubric, the game board when the suite has one, the answer, a summary of the answer's tool use, and the claim verifier's rulings on the answer's claims. With Blind on (this profile: {{ blind }}), it does not see the assessor's score, levels, comments, critical-error flag or accusations, so its verdict is a genuinely independent reading. With Blind off, it sees the assessor's verdict and tends to agree with it.

What it checks. The whole verdict, graded from scratch on the same scale: the four levels, the quality score, and whether the answer contains a critical error.

What it produces.

  • Per answer: its own verdict, shown under the assessor's. When the two quality scores differ by more than 15 points, or one finds a critical error and the other does not, the answer is marked DISPUTED and listed in the report's Disputed Assessments.
  • Per run: Assessor Agreement: the mean absolute and signed difference between the two, the critical-error splits, and how many answers disagree. What this figure means depends on coverage.

How it affects grading. It does not change any score. The assessor's verdict always counts, so the Intelligence Index, charts and item statistics are the same whatever the second reader says. A DISPUTED mark is a task for you: open the answer, read both verdicts, and if the assessor was wrong, use Re-assess Question. A re-assessment is what changes a score.

Cost and time. One extra grading call for each answer it reads. It runs inside the per-answer pipeline, so it adds little wall time, except the outlier sweep's final stage.

Comparability. The second reader's model and settings, its coverage, Blind and the minimum sample are part of the run's instrument. Changing them changes no score, but multi-run analysis and Model Comparison count it as an instrument difference. Keep them the same across runs you intend to compare.

3. Choosing coverage (single-assessor runs)

No coverage setting changes a score. Coverage decides how much of the grading is checked, and what the agreement figure can tell you.

Coverage Which answers are read What the agreement figure means Cost Use it when
Never None No figure and no DISPUTED marks. The report states how many answers a trigger would have picked None Grading cost matters more than any check on the assessor
Only flagged answers Answers that raised a flag (list below) Agreement on doubtful verdicts only: it says nothing about grading reliability in general, and is empty when the candidate does well Lowest You want a cheap safety net on suspicious verdicts
Flagged answers and statistical outliers Flagged, plus answers more than {{ delta }} points below the run's median score, found after scoring As above Low; adds a final stage You suspect the assessor is harsh on a few answers without flagging them
Flagged answers plus a sample Flagged, topped up to {{ sample }} answers with the lowest scores, the same answers every time for the same data As above, plus a small low-score sample, so there is always a figure Low, bounded Routine and diagnostic runs; the Standard profile's default
Every answer (double grading) — Recommended All Unbiased grading reliability, the figure to quote. Also catches a confidently wrong verdict that raised no flag, the error every trigger misses One call per answer Runs used to compare or select models

The flags. An answer is flagged, in this order, when:

  1. the assessor found a critical error;
  2. the claim verifier refuted one of its claims (needs a claim verifier);
  3. the assessor's comment describes a defect its verdict does not reflect;
  4. accuracy was docked on the assessor's own knowledge ("not in rubric") or on a claim it could not verify;
  5. accuracy was docked with no defect named;
  6. an omission was docked as an accuracy error;
  7. one dimension collapsed while the others are high, with no reason given;
  8. unverifiable claims come with low accuracy;
  9. the quality score is below {{ threshold }}.

4. Turning the second reader off

Choosing None (or Coverage Never) leaves every score, index and chart exactly as it would otherwise be. You give up:

  • the DISPUTED marks;
  • the agreement figures;
  • the only check that catches an assessor that is confidently wrong.

The report still counts how many answers would have been read. A run without a second reader differs from one with it in one instrument key.

5. The Reference Reader (panel runs)

Why it changes role. With a co-assessor, two graders already score every answer, and their agreement is measured (Panel Agreement, MEMBERS DISAGREE). A second reader for agreement would only repeat that.

What question it answers. "Is the panel balanced between its members' own families?" Models tend to favor answers written by their own family. The panel's average cancels this only if both members favor their own family equally. The reference reader comes from a third family and grades every answer blind. Model Comparison then compares:

  • the gap between two candidate families under the panel;
  • the same gap under the reader (the asymmetry estimate).

It also flags pairs of models that the panel and the reader rank in opposite order.

What else it catches. An answer is marked DISPUTED when the reader is more than 15 points from the panel score, or disagrees with a critical-error verdict both members share. That is the case of both members being wrong the same way.

Coverage. Always every answer, blind. Not configurable.

How it affects grading. None. The panel average is the score.

Choosing the model. A family that is neither a panel member nor, ideally, a candidate. The same model and settings on every panel run you intend to compare.

Leaving it out. Scores are unchanged. You lose the balance check, the reader's index, reference-dependent pairs and its DISPUTED marks, and runs with and without it differ in one instrument key.

Limits. The reader is assumed neutral between the members' families. If it spreads its scores more narrowly than the panel, the balance check absorbs part of the real difference between families. With few questions its intervals are wide, so read it over several suites.

6. The Claim Verifier

In one sentence. When a grader says a fact in an answer is wrong, or that it cannot be sure, the claim verifier looks the fact up in the game's source code and wiki and rules on it: true (Supported), false (Refuted) or undecided (Indeterminate).

What question it answers. "Was the grader right about this fact?"

When it runs. For each answer, right after the grading. It only runs when a grader raised something it could check:

  • a claim it could not verify;
  • a passage it called a critical error;
  • a fact it used to dock accuracy that is not in the rubric;
  • a sentence it called false.

An answer with no such charge costs nothing. In a panel run it checks both members' charges in one pass.

What it has. Read-only access to the same game knowledge the chat assistant uses: the GnollHack and NetHack wikis, the knowledge base, the game source code, and the monster, item and artifact data. It also sees the question, the rubric, the game board, the disputed passages and the grader's reasoning. Every ruling must cite where it found the answer. A ruling without a valid citation counts as undecided.

How it affects grading. It never changes a score, level or critical-error cap. What it does is make grading mistakes visible:

  • Marks on the answer.
    • Refuted claim: the answer states something false. The second reader looks at these first.
    • Contested critical error: the passage called an error is actually true.
    • Contested accuracy deduction: a deduction rested on something false, or docked a true sentence.
  • A "what if" score (single-assessor runs). For a contested answer, the assessor grades once more with the evidence in hand. The report shows how much the index would move. The published score stays as it was.
  • Report lines that show how much of the score rests on deductions the evidence does not support, and a Claim Verification Yield line with claims checked, found false, cost per claim, and share of the run's cost.
  • The second or reference reader sees its rulings.
  • Panel runs. An accusation audit: how often each member's charges turned out wrong, per candidate family. It is a second check that the panel is fair to both families.
  • Suite Health. False facts in answers become suggested knowledge-base topics. True facts missing from a rubric become suggested rubric additions.

Why use it if it does not change scores? Because graders get facts wrong too, and without a verifier nobody sees it. The score is quietly too low or too high, and nothing marks the answer. In this benchmark's own history, a verifier-style check has shown that:

  • 3 of 5 published critical errors on one series of runs were the grader's mistake;
  • one assessor called true facts "fabricated" on 7 of 18 answers;
  • another docked statements the source code confirms word for word.

The verifier tells you:

  • which answers to re-assess. Re-assessing is what changes a score;
  • which rubrics are missing facts;
  • which facts the chat assistant gets wrong, the most useful input for improving the assistant's knowledge base.

Its limits. It is an AI model too, and it is sometimes wrong: dozens of wrong rulings are recorded to date. Treat a ruling as strong evidence for your review, not as the final word.

Cost. It is usually the most expensive grading role, often 40–55 % of a run's cost, even on a low-cost model, because each check makes several lookups. A run whose graders raise few charges spends little on it.

Use it when:

  • the run will be analyzed, published, or used to choose a model;
  • its findings will feed chat or knowledge-base improvements;
  • it is a panel run (the accusation audit needs it);
  • the suite or its rubrics are new or were just rewritten.

Skip it when:

  • it is a quick smoke test, or a re-run that checks only speed, cost or a UI change;
  • the suite is large, the budget is tight, and only the score matters.

Leaving it out. Scores are unchanged. The report says None selected and counts the claims left unchecked. You lose everything under How it affects grading, and a run without a verifier is counted as a different instrument from one with it.

7. Choosing grader models

The principle. Where the score is decided, use a strong model, but not the provider's most expensive one: grading has not needed the top tier, and it costs several times as much. Where the role only checks, use a fast, low-cost model. Use medium thinking or reasoning effort everywhere:

  • low effort makes careless graders;
  • the highest levels (xhigh, max) have made grading much slower and caused broken, unreadable verdicts, with no gain seen.
Role Model Not this Thinking or reasoning effort Also
Assessor Claude Opus Claude Fable, about 2.5 times the price Medium Never the model under test
Co-Assessor GPT Sol GPT Astra, about 5 times the price Medium, the same as the assessor Never the model under test: when the candidate is the newest GPT Sol, use the previous Sol version. A weaker member would make half of every score noisier
Second Reader Gemini Flash Flash-Lite, a lighter tier not tried for grading Medium If the candidate is a Gemini Flash model, use a different Flash version
Reference Reader Gemini Flash Flash-Lite Medium Google is the third family beside the two panel members. Use the same model on every run you compare
Claim Verifier Gemini Flash Flash-Lite Medium; high if its rulings are often wrong; never low It must use the lookup tools reliably
Report writer Claude Opus or GPT Sol, from a family other than the model under test Claude Fable or GPT Astra: prose for readers does not need the top tier Medium; high if its documents often need repair; never low Never the model under test (refused); preferably not its family

The final summary uses the assessor's model and effort. Use the current version of each family, kept the same across the runs you compare. Changing a grader's version makes later runs a different instrument.

Why these choices. In this benchmark the grader has moved the headline score more than the difference between the models being tested: one set of runs scored about 70 under one assessor and 97 under a weaker one. A weaker assessor has also missed claims that needed checking, which left the verifier nothing to do. Opus and Sol are strong enough for this; the tier above them costs far more for no gain the benchmark has seen. The checking roles never score, so a cheaper model there changes cost, not results. The verifier is the largest cost of a run, so a low-cost model there saves the most.
